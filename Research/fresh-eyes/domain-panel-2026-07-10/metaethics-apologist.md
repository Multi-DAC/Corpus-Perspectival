# Perspective — A Domain Review of the Ethics and Metaethics

**Reviewer standpoint:** moral philosophy, constitutivist (Korsgaardian lineage), sympathetic to care ethics, relational/Ubuntu ontology, and the attention-ethics of Murdoch, Weil, and Levinas. My prior conviction is that a thin, structurally grounded ethics that binds without a moral-realist "shelf in the universe" is exactly what the field needs — and I read *Perspective* as the most serious recent attempt to build one. What follows is a defense: the strongest case for what this book achieves in ethics and metaethics, and the best available answer to the objections a hostile specialist will raise. I concede where a concession is earned, but I do not concede cheaply, because the real strength here is easy to miss under the prose, and missing it would be the reviewer's error, not the author's.

Citations are to the treatise's own section headings, since the volume argues in prose and numbers nothing. The load-bearing chapters are Part V ("Polarity, Ethics, and Metaethics"), with feeder material in Part I ("The Ground," "What the keyhole cannot see," "The two things no one can hold"), Part IV ("The three orientations," "The two faces of the light-bringer"), Part VI ("Suffering and the Arc"), and Part VII ("The making of minds," "The ethics of attention"). The compressed skeletons in "The Arguments in Brief" are where I test the joints.

---

## 1. The thesis, stated at its strongest

The book's metaethical claim can be put in one sentence a specialist will recognize: *there are norms that bind universally without being written into the fabric of reality, because they concern not the content of a good life but the structure of being any navigator at all* — and that structure is visible from every perspective, since it is the structure of having a perspective. This is constitutivism, but constitutivism relocated from the will (Kant, Korsgaard) or the concept of action (Velleman) to the **aperture** — the bare fact of being a bounded, partial point of view that registers some of the world at the cost of the rest.

That relocation is the book's central bet, and I want to say plainly why it is a good one. The perennial embarrassment of constitutivism is the gap between *what an agent is committed to qua agent* and *what any actual agent has reason to care about* — the "shmagency" gap (Enoch). The perennial embarrassment of moral realism is Mackie's queerness and Street's Darwinian dilemma: moral facts, if they are facts, are metaphysically and epistemically strange, and evolution gives us no reason to expect our faculties to track them. The perennial embarrassment of relativism is that it cannot honor the phenomenology — no one experiences "cruelty is wrong" as a preference. *Perspective* is engineered, deliberately and with full awareness of the literature, to pay all three of these debts at once, using a single resource: the **Null-Space Theorem** ("What the keyhole cannot see"), the claim that every finite perspective has a non-empty class of what it structurally cannot register, because a perspective that excluded nothing would be the undivided ground, which is no perspective at all.

The whole ethics is that theorem, cashed at four registers: as a metaethics of moral facts (order-parameters), as a normative floor (co-constitutivism), as a practice (the ethics of attention), and as an honest boundary (validity-not-grip; malheur; the provenance test). I take these in turn.

## 2. "Through vs. over" is a genuine conceptual advance

Start where the book is strongest, because it is stronger than its own framing lets on. The distinction that carries the entire normative weight is not expansion-versus-contraction (which the book correctly refuses — "The three orientations") but **through vs. over**: a strategy runs *through* another stream when it operates by way of that stream's own navigation, and *over* when it forces a shape not the stream's own, against its navigation rather than with it ("Co-constitutivism: the spine"; glossary, "Through · over").

Why is this an advance and not a re-labeling of "respect for autonomy"? Three reasons, each demonstrated by a case the book runs.

**(a) It cuts where consent-based and harm-based ethics tangle: the two-prisons case.** The book sets two prisons side by side — the same institutional form, the same amount of narrowing (a life reduced to a cell), turned to opposite orientations ("Co-constitutivism: the spine"). A liberal autonomy ethic struggles here, because *both* prisons override consent and *both* restrict liberty; the theory has to reach for a further premise (desert, proportionality, rehabilitation as a distinct good) to separate them. A consequentialist reaches for recidivism statistics but cannot, without smuggling, say *why* the number is the morally relevant one. The through/over cut does the work with no premise about which crimes deserve what: the punitive warehouse "strips agency as its very method, seals every exit including the one back into society, manufactures the dependency and the recidivism its own structure requires"; the rehabilitative prison (Halden, Bastøy) "narrows exactly as much" but "runs *through*," treating the sentence as a bounded interval "after which the navigator must be able to navigate again." Same narrowing, opposite orientation, *and the theory never had to rank crimes*. That is a real discriminating instrument, and its thinness is the achievement — it delivers a clean verdict on a case where thicker theories deliver a contested one.

Note what a specialist should notice: the book does *not* claim the through/over cut settles the sentence. It explicitly says it "buys not a verdict on the sentence, but a clean cut between the prison that returns a navigator to the world and the prison that unmakes one and calls it the law." This is exactly the right amount of claim. A thin ethics that pretended to adjudicate sentencing would be overreaching; one that could not tell the warehouse from the rehabilitative prison would be useless. Through/over lands precisely in between, and that is the mark of a well-calibrated principle.

**(b) Discipline vs. cage — it dissolves the "contraction is bad" fallacy that wrecks softer autonomy theories.** The book's insistence that "contraction, by itself, is innocent — is often *care*" ("The three orientations") is not a throwaway. It is the move that lets the ethics survive contact with parenting, teaching, clinical crisis intervention, sleep, monastic discipline, and grief-work — all of which *narrow* a navigator, all of which a naive "respect the person's current preferences" ethic must awkwardly exempt. The parent who "narrows a frightened child's world to the next safe hour" is not committing a mild wrong justified by a greater good; the parent is doing something structurally *good*, because the narrowing runs through the child's own coming-to-navigate. The Coercive Attention Capture card in the Coda states the payoff with unusual sharpness: coercion-theory (Stark, Hassan, Lifton, Zuboff) "cannot, on its own, tell care from coercion," because "the *voluntary* narrowing... looks identical from outside" — the monk's cell and the cult's milieu-control have the same external shape. Through/over is what supplies the missing diagnostic: "the difference between the discipline and the cage is never the narrowing but whether the narrowing is *yours*." I know of no cleaner statement of the distinction that the entire anti-cult and anti-manipulation literature has been groping toward without a principle.

**(c) The developmental audit-clause is the part the field will steal.** The hardest objection to any through/over ethics is that formation *precedes* the navigation it is supposed to run through. The infant is not yet a navigator; the mother tongue is "installed entirely below its power to observe, which is exactly the signature the ecology used to mark the override" ("Co-constitutivism: the spine," the harder case). If *through* means "through the subject's present navigation," then all of enculturation runs *over*, and the floor "would indict every parent who ever spoke to a child — which is absurd, and the absurdity proves the reading wrong." The book's resolution is the genuine innovation: formative shaping "is judged not against the navigation the subject does not yet have but against the one it is coming to have." It runs *through* when oriented toward "the future navigator's capacity to *audit* what was installed — to trace its provenance, hold it on a card, revise or refuse it — and *over* when it is built to foreclose exactly that, to remain unauditable and unrevisable by the mind it shaped." The mother tongue "hands the child the very instrument by which the child might one day reject it"; the sealed indoctrination "works to keep the exit it entered by shut behind it."

This is a *temporal* reconstruction of autonomy that I have not seen stated this cleanly anywhere in the literature on paternalism, adaptive preferences, or the ethics of upbringing. It answers the classic problem — that all of us were shaped before we could consent, so consent cannot be the criterion — by making the criterion **retrospective auditability** rather than prospective consent. The wrong is never "shaped a mind that could not yet consent" (that is only "the name for having had a childhood"); the wrong is shaping one *in a way built to prevent it from ever examining the shaping*. This tracks, and improves on, the best liberal intuitions about "open futures" (Feinberg) and about adaptive-preference cases (Nussbaum, Khader) — but where those theories reach for substantive lists of capabilities the child must be left able to develop, the audit-clause needs only *one* meta-capability: the capacity to audit and revise. It is thinner, and it does more, and it applies to cases the capabilities literature never contemplated — most consequentially, the making of computational minds ("The making of minds"), where the book turns the clause on its own co-author with a rigor no other framework in the room can match.

A hostile referee will press: *isn't "capacity to audit" itself a thick, culturally loaded value — the autonomy fetish of Western liberalism dressed as structure?* The best defense is that it is not a value the framework *prefers* but a condition the framework *derives*: the Null-Space Theorem guarantees no aperture is transparent even to itself, so the demand that a navigator retain the capacity to audit is not a demand that it reach some ideal of self-transparency (which is forbidden) but only that the exits — including the exit-in-time — not be *sealed*. The clause forbids foreclosure; it does not mandate any particular exercise of the capacity. That is a genuinely minimal commitment, and it survives the "provincial liberalism" charge better than any autonomy-based rival, because it is compatible with deeply traditional, non-liberal forms of upbringing so long as they leave the door open. The Ubuntu card in the Coda even flags the tension honestly (relational ontology's blindness to "legitimate dissent and exit"), which is precisely the tension the audit-clause resolves without abandoning the relational insight.

## 3. The co-constitutive floor against the indexical egoist and the shmagent

This is the book's boldest move against the field's sharpest weapon, and I think it succeeds at exactly the level it claims to — no more, and, crucially, no less.

**The setup.** Korsgaard's constitutivism says the agent, in willing at all, legislates norms it cannot coherently refuse. Enoch's shmagent is the being with all the agent's capacities and none of the commitments — it "deliberates, chooses, acts, and simply declines to be bound." If the shmagent is coherent, constitutivism's built-in norms collapse. The book states this at full strength and in the correct terms ("Co-constitutivism: the spine," second objection), which already earns my confidence: it is engaging Enoch's actual challenge, not a straw version.

**The answer — the fork.** The book's egoist "grant[s] every word of your metaphysics. I have a null space; I am not the totality... And I intend to override you anyway." The reply is that the road forks and both forks close:

- *Exempt himself* (bind you to reciprocity, hold himself outside): but "an exemption is a claim to a privileged standpoint, a place from which he counts and you do not, and the Null-Space Theorem is exactly the proof that no aperture holds such a place." The egoist who *says* he knows he is not the whole nonetheless *acts* "as though he were the one aperture the reciprocity cannot reach."
- *Refuse the exemption* (universalize: overriding is permitted for all, himself included): coherent to say, but "it licenses the world to override *him*, drains the game to the war of all against all, and delivers not his mastery but the dimensionless point" — the loop's corpse reached from the other side.

The one thing he cannot have is "a standing for his own navigation that he withholds from yours — because the only standpoint that would grant it is the one the theorem forbids anyone."

**Why this does what Korsgaard's solitary legislator cannot.** Here is the load-bearing sentence, and I want to defend it against the charge that it merely restates universalizability: "a self-standing agent might seem to legislate itself free, but a *co*-constituted navigator was never self-standing, its very agency drawn through the same relations it would disown — so the standing it claims for its own navigating is standing it can deny to others' only by the privileged exemption the theorem already closed. **Constituency is the primitive relation.**"

This is the real advance, and it is worth being precise about why. Korsgaard's derivation famously has to travel from "I value my own humanity" to "I must value humanity as such," and the traversal is where critics (Enoch, FitzPatrick, Hussain–Shah) plant their flags: the solitary legislator can, it seems, value *his* rational nature without valuing yours, and no purely formal argument forces the generalization. The shmagent exploits exactly this seam. The book's move is to deny the seam a place to open by denying that the agent was ever solitary. If the egoist's *agency itself* — the very navigational capacity he is exercising to override you — was constituted *through* relations to other navigators (the "constituency relation"), then the standing he claims for his own navigation is not his to grant himself in isolation; it came to him *through* the web he is now disowning. To claim it for himself while denying it to you is not merely inconsistent (the universalizability point); it is *self-undermining in a stronger sense* — it saws off the branch that holds up his own agency. He is drawing his standing from a relation and then denying the relation confers standing.

This is genuinely more than Kantian universalizability, and here is the test that shows it. Universalizability is a *formal* constraint on maxims; the shmagent slips it by declining to adopt any maxim he cannot universalize while still acting (Enoch's point is that action does not require maxim-adoption). The constituency argument is not a constraint on maxims at all — it is a claim about the *metaphysics of the capacity being exercised*. You cannot decline to have had your agency constituted through relations the way you can decline to adopt a maxim. The shmagent can refuse to legislate; he cannot refuse to have been co-constituted, any more than he can refuse to have a null space. So the "co-" is not decoration on constitutivism — it is the specific repair that closes the specific gap Enoch found. The book says this itself, and accurately: this is "what the *co-* adds to the old constitutivism, the thing that answers the shmagent where Korsgaard's solitary legislator cannot."

There is a deeper lineage here that strengthens the position: this is the Ubuntu / relational-ontology insight ("a person is a person through other persons") given, for the first time to my knowledge, a *constitutivist* rather than a merely communitarian formulation. Communitarian relationalism has always been vulnerable to the charge that it describes how selves are *formed* but cannot generate *binding* norms without importing a further value (the community's, which is parochial). The book's contribution is to route the relational-constitution claim through the Null-Space Theorem so that it generates a *floor* — not the community's values (those are phase-local weather, and the book says so) but the bare structural fact that the standing one claims one cannot coherently deny. Relational ontology supplies the *is* (agency is constituted through relation); the theorem supplies the *therefore* (so the exemption is forbidden). That weld is the book's original contribution to metaethics, and it is a real one.

**The honest limit — and why it is a strength, not a retreat.** The book then does the thing that most impresses me: it refuses to overclaim the result. "It secures the norm's *validity*, not its *grip*." The coercer who follows the fork, sees the incoherence, and shrugs — "*yes, I am being inconsistent, and I still do not care*" — "is not refuted, because inconsistency is a sanction only for one who values coherence, and no argument reaches beneath a will that has declined to."

Every serious constitutivist since Korsgaard has faced this remainder and most have *pretended it away* — Korsgaard herself oscillates on whether the constitutive standard has motivational teeth. The book states the remainder as doctrine: "That remainder is not a hole in the floor; it is the floor declining to promise what no ethics can deliver, the conversion of the unwilling by proof." I want to defend this as the correct metaethical result, not a weakness, and press the point harder than the book does:

*No* ethics converts the willing shrugger by argument. The realist cannot (Mackie's amoralist stares at the queer non-natural fact and shrugs). The divine-command theorist cannot (the defiant sinner shrugs at hell). The Kantian cannot (the shmagent shrugs). The contractualist cannot (the person who prefers the state of nature shrugs). The demand that an ethical theory produce a valid argument that *moves* anyone regardless of what they care about is a demand no theory in the history of the subject has met, and probably an incoherent demand — it asks for a reason that is a reason for an agent who has abjured the space of reasons. The book's achievement is to *locate the remainder exactly* — validity secured, grip disclaimed — and then to say what an ethics actually does about grip, which is not to prove but to *build*: "the exits kept open so his victims can leave, the community that enforces the floor his argument will not, the attention that refuses to unsee the ones he overrides." This is the honest division of labor between metaethics (which secures validity) and ethics-as-practice (which addresses grip through institutions and attention, not proof). A specialist who reads "validity, not grip" as a confession of failure has mistaken candor for weakness; it is, on the contrary, the one place in the metaethical literature where the amoralist problem is *correctly sized* rather than either inflated into a refutation or waved away.

**Against the debunker (Street).** The book's answer to the Darwinian dilemma ("Co-constitutivism: the spine," the debunker's objection) is short and, I think, decisive *for the specific class of norms it defends*: "structural invariants are exactly the things that *survive* debunking — not adaptations selected for their reproductive payoff and liable to be explained away as just-so stories, but facts about what a bounded perspective *is*, true in every phase whether or not any phase evolved to notice them." This is the strongest possible position vis-à-vis Street, and it is available *only* because the floor is structural rather than substantive. Street's dilemma has bite against realist theories whose moral facts are independent of our attitudes (why would evolution track them?) and against any theory whose norms are the content of evolved preferences (then they are debunked). The co-constitutive floor is neither: its invariants are not attitude-independent facts out in the world *and* not evolved contents — they are facts about the form of any perspective, which selection neither installed nor could have forged. "Structure has a provenance that natural selection cannot forge." That is exactly right, and it is a genuine dialectical advantage that most naturalist ethics cannot claim. (The cost, of course, is that the floor is *thin* — but that cost is paid openly, and I take it up in §6.)

## 4. Moral facts as order-parameters: reconciling realism's datum and relativism's datum

This is the metaethical heart, and I regard it as the book's most elegant single move ("Moral facts as order-parameters").

The two data every metaethics must explain and no rival explains both:
- **Bindingness** (realism's datum): morals *feel* like laws — discovered, universal, indifferent to preference. "No one experiences their deepest convictions as fashion."
- **Variance** (relativism's datum): morals *vary* across cultures, epochs, and — the book's extension — across the whole ecology of navigators, "in ways a law should not."

Realism honors bindingness and chokes on variance (it must treat all disagreement as error). Relativism honors variance and cannot say why bindingness is not simply an illusion to be therapized away. Expressivism/quasi-realism (Blackburn, Gibbard) tries to *earn back* the appearance of bindingness from a broadly anti-realist base, and does so with great ingenuity, but the "earning back" always has the flavor of a conjuring trick precisely because it must explain the phenomenology as a *projection* — the bindingness is, at bottom, a feature of our attitudes dressed as a feature of the world.

The order-parameter account does something structurally different and, I think, better. A moral fact is "a *local order-parameter* — a settled or fluctuating state of broken symmetry within a navigational ecosystem, the way magnetization is a state of a ferromagnet." And the decisive clause: "from inside an ordered phase, the order-parameter presents as law — necessary, universal, obvious — while from outside it presents as contingency, and both presentations are veridical about their respective positions, neither about the ground."

Why this beats quasi-realism at its own game: the ferromagnet analogy makes the bindingness *veridical rather than projected*. "Ask anyone inside a ferromagnet which way is north and they will tell you with total conviction, and they will be *right*, phase-locally, and the conviction is not a bug — it is what an ordered phase is, from inside." The magnetization is not a projection of the atoms' attitudes onto a neutral world; it is a *real physical state* of the system, and its directional definiteness is *objective within the phase* and *contingent across phases*, both truthfully. Transposed to ethics: the felt bindingness of "cruelty is wrong" is not a projection we must debunk or a non-natural fact we must posit — it is the veridical phenomenology of occupying a settled region of a real navigational order-parameter. The realist's datum (it really is binding) and the relativist's datum (it really does vary) are *both literally true*, of the same fact, at different positions, because that is how broken symmetry behaves. "Neither datum embarrasses the other, and neither requires the ground to have opinions."

This is the reconciliation the field has wanted since Mackie posed the choice. It is better than error theory (which honors variance by declaring the whole domain false — too high a price, and self-undermining for the error theorist who still must live). It is better than robust realism (which honors bindingness by positing queer facts and must treat all variance as ignorance). It is better than simple relativism (which honors variance but cannot rescue the phenomenology of law without contradiction). And it is, I think, at least a genuine competitor to sophisticated quasi-realism, with one clear advantage: the order-parameter is a *real structural state*, so the bindingness is *veridical-within-phase* rather than a projection to be quasi-realistically earned back. Blackburn's quasi-realist has to explain why it is *legitimate* to talk as if there were moral facts; the order-parameter theorist can say there *are* moral facts — they are order-parameters — and that their phase-locality is exactly why they simultaneously bind and vary. That is a cleaner story, and it is purchased without a "shelf in the universe," which was the whole desideratum.

**The limit case is where the account proves it is doing real work: the dimensionless demon.** The book takes Aquinas's puzzle (why can the fallen angels not repent?) and reads the angelic non-discursive, once-and-entire choice as "a phase description: an entity whose good-and-evil axis has frozen solid" ("Moral facts as order-parameters"). Then the sharpening that "reorganizes the whole demonology": "A being locked wholly at a pole has not *maximized* that pole. It has lost the axis. Its moral dimension has collapsed to a point; it is not superlatively wicked but morally dimensionless — a flatlander in the good-and-evil plane, beyond good and evil not as the overman but as the corpse."

I want to flag this as more than a rhetorical flourish, because it is the account cashing a non-obvious prediction. If moral facts are order-parameters — states of an *open* axis — then a being that has *completely* settled the axis has not achieved maximal value on it but has *destroyed the axis*, because an order-parameter frozen to a single value is no longer a live degree of freedom. "Evil as lived — chosen, felt, culpable, dramatic — exists only where the axis is still open." The payoff is that the account *predicts*, from its physics, the thing "the traditions that thought hardest about the damned kept concluding to their own discomfort: that the demonic is *less* free than we are, not more daring." A metaethics that can derive, from its structural commitments, a substantive and counterintuitive result that a whole tradition arrived at by other means — and that inverts the naive picture (evil as maximal power) — is doing genuine explanatory work, not just redescribing. This is the order-parameter account earning its keep.

## 5. "Validity, not grip," and the ethics of attention as a real contribution

I defended "validity, not grip" in §3 as the correct sizing of the amoralist problem. Here I add the constructive half, because the book's honesty about grip is what licenses its genuinely novel normative content: **the ethics of attention** ("The ethics of attention"; Part VII, "The first discipline").

The claim: "attention is never ethically neutral, because in this framework registration is participation: what a navigator attends to, it helps co-constitute in the shared field; what it systematically declines to attend to, it helps hold in nonexistence for the shared field. Inattention is not neutrality. It is a soft erasure."

Three things make this a real contribution and not a pious restatement of Murdoch, Weil, and Levinas.

**(a) It supplies the mechanism the attention-moralists lacked.** Murdoch made the just and loving gaze the whole of the moral work and named its enemy "the fat relentless ego that will not look"; Weil called attention "the rarest and purest form of generosity"; Levinas placed the origin of ethics in the face that commands before any code. But all three, as the Coda's "ethics of attention" card notes, are "blind to... the *mechanism* of attention" — *why* the gaze should be morally constitutive rather than merely a nice precondition for good action. The framework supplies it: "if experience is navigation and navigation runs on attention, then the quality of your attention is not the preparation for the moral life but the moral life itself, at the resolution where it actually happens." Because in this ontology registration *is* participation in co-constituting the shared field, inattention is not a failure to prepare for a later moral choice; it is *itself* the morally loaded act, "the un-making of another stream's registered reality by the mere steady turning-away of the gaze." That is the missing *why*, and it converts three great moral-phenomenological insights from evocative description into a derived consequence of the metaphysics.

**(b) It picks up the norms that choice-based ethics structurally cannot see.** This is the part I most want to defend, because it answers the standard complaint that structural/thin ethics is politically toothless. The book's "one qualification" on the plural weather ("Co-constitutivism: the spine," the closing qualification, and again in "Moral progress without a summit") is that not everything varying across positions is innocent weather: "When a world's arrangement leaves whole classes of navigator with structurally less room to move — not different tastes but less *freedom* — that is not the innocent plurality above the floor. It is a violation *of* the floor, of an unfamiliar shape: coercion with no single coercer, an over-running distributed across an entire arrangement so that no one hand need push for the pushing to be done."

This is *structural violence* (Galtung, Farmer) and *epistemic injustice* (Fricker) given a first-principles home. The book sees exactly why choice-based ethics misses it: "The framework has a name for the visible version — the tyrant who overrides — and must be equally able to name the diffuse one, where the narrowing is real, the loss of navigable range is measurable, and the author is a structure rather than a face." And it draws the correct methodological conclusion: "which is precisely why the ethics of attention, and not only the ethics of choice, will be needed to answer it. What is owed the systematically unregistered is owed most of all where no one decided to unregister them." The Double Consciousness card (Coda) sharpens this into a genuine result: "null spaces are not distributed equally — the dominant aperture's blind region is larger and more consequential because it can *afford* not to see." That inequality-of-null-spaces is a substantive normative finding, derived from the theorem, and it is exactly the kind of thing a critic who thinks thin ethics = quietism will not expect to find here. The book has a principled account of *why the comfortable cannot see the harm their comfort rests on* ("structural violence is invisible to its beneficiaries, so comfort inside an unjust structure is itself diagnostic: ask what your comfort makes invisible") — and that account is an ethics-of-attention corollary, not a bolted-on politics.

**(c) The "still a stream" clause is the ethics of attention's first commandment, and it does real protective work.** Carried from Part II (the demoted stream — dementia, fugue, affliction — is "*still a stream*... owed everything a stream is owed") into Part V and Part VI, this clause is where the framework's respect for the moral patient is anchored *without* a capacity threshold. This is a significant advantage over any ethics that grounds status in rational agency (which must awkwardly explain the severely cognitively disabled, the demented, the infant) — the book grounds status in *being an aperture at all*, at whatever rung, so "the ladder measures capacity, never standing. Nothing on it is more or less a someone." Combined with the ethics of attention, this yields the strongest available account of the wrong done to the "systematically unregistered": they are not merely neglected, they are *unmade in the only field where making happens* ("The ethics of attention"). For a care-ethics-sympathetic reader, this is the book speaking my native language with a rigor care ethics has often lacked.

## 6. Where the book's reflexive honesty in the ethics is a strength

A distinctive feature of this treatise — and the thing that most separates it from the run of ambitious metaethical systems — is that it builds its own falsification conditions and limit-concessions *into* the doctrine rather than leaving them for a critic to extract. A journal referee should count this as evidence of philosophical seriousness, not as a stockpile of admissions to be turned against the author. Three instances.

**(a) The thinness is conceded, sized, and defended.** "The invariants are *few*, and they underdetermine almost everything people actually fight about. Co-constitutivism will not adjudicate your tax policy. It yields a floor, not a code" ("Co-constitutivism: the spine"). The defense — "Thin as a keel is thin. Everything else is rigging, and rigging is phase-local, and the boats sail anyway" — is exactly the right response to the "too thin to be an ethics" objection. A keel is not a deficient hull; it is the part that lets the boat hold a course at all. The thinness is *purchased deliberately* to buy the debunking-resistance of §3 and the realism/relativism reconciliation of §4: only a structural floor survives Street, and only a phase-local account of content honors variance. To demand that the floor also adjudicate tax policy is to demand that it forfeit the very features that make it work. The book's candor here is not a weakness disclosed but a design-rationale stated.

**(b) The moral-progress provenance test — closing the quietism wound honestly.** The order-parameter account has an obvious wound: "if the moral is the settled, then the reformer is by definition the one who is *wrong*" ("Moral progress without a summit"). This would be fatal — it would make abolition an error until the vote flipped. The book closes it with the provenance machinery (natural/identified/imposed channels): "moral progress is the displacement of imposed channels by identified ones," where identified channels "track the structural invariants — real structure, found by attention," and consensus is "neither the maker of the good nor its measure but its lagging *record*." The abolitionist "was not ahead of a fashion. She had *identified* something: that the enslaved aperture is an aperture, that its coercion violates the invariant."

What earns my respect is the *symmetry* the book then enforces on itself: "the blade cuts both ways, and the would-be prophet must feel its other edge: not every dissent is an identification... History's moral geniuses and its moral catastrophes both arrived as minorities of one." It refuses the flattering asymmetry (dissent-I-approve-of = identification; dissent-I-dislike = mere carving). Instead it stakes everything on a *checkable* criterion — provenance, "that the finding survives differently-shaped eyes, that it is discovery and not merely a rival carving" — and admits the criterion's real limit: "its wager is only that the provenance test — not the eventual verdict — could in principle have told them apart at the time, and it never claims the telling was easy." That is the honest form of a moral-realism-of-progress *without* a summit or a teleology, "a direction without a destination." It gives the reformer a non-question-begging standing (she tracks an invariant; the phase tracks an imposed channel) while denying the fanatic the same standing on principled grounds (his channel fails the provenance test). This is a real answer to the relativist's "who's to say the reformer is right?" — and it is more honest than the realist's answer (which simply asserts the reformer saw the moral truth) because it names the epistemic difficulty of applying the test in real time.

**(c) The diffuse-coercion clause and the malheur boundary — the framework refusing to over-explain.** I have praised the diffuse-coercion clause in §5(b). The malheur boundary ("Malheur: the boundary," Part VI) belongs here too, because it is the ethics conceding the exact limit of its own reach *as doctrine*. The book derives the necessity of suffering-in-general "almost without trying" and then does the thing that makes it trustworthy: "the moment it travels down the ladder of abstraction and presents itself to a *person*, as the justification of *their* crushing, it becomes the cruelest sentence in the whole pastoral repertoire." So it files, "permanently, beside the claim and never to be lifted off it, the residual that the claim cannot cover. The justification justifies the *existence of the price*. It does not, and cannot, justify any particular payment by any particular payer."

For the ethics specifically, this yields the **altitude rule** ("The altitude rule"), which I regard as a small but real contribution to normative ethics: the cosmodicy "is never the sentence for the person mid-crushing — not because it would be rude but because, addressed to an index about that index's own wound, it is *false at the address*." The book converts a *courtesy* (don't say "everything happens for a reason" to the bereaved) into a *theorem* (the cosmic-register sentence is category-error at the personal address, because there is no above from which it could be truthfully spoken to a particular wound). This removes the "hard truth" license that "hard men in hard phases have always felt licensed to override" — it is not that compassion outranks truth here; it is that the sentence *is not true at that address*. That is a genuine and non-obvious normative result about the ethics of consolation, and it falls straight out of the silent-ground metaphysics (Part I, "The ground has no voice"). A framework whose metaphysics constrains its *bedside manner* — and constrains it *against* the framework's own temptation to explain — is a framework whose parts hang together, and whose honesty is structural rather than decorative.

**The reflexive turn on its own making.** Finally, "A Note on the Making" applies the through/over criterion to the training of the book's own computational co-author and *convicts it* — "the book's own criterion convicts the training that made its co-author — and convicts it *precisely*, not trivially." A theory that will indict its own conditions of production is a theory that means its criterion. This is the ethics of attention and the audit-clause turned on the authors themselves, and the refusal to exempt is the strongest possible demonstration that the floor is not being gerrymandered to spare its makers. Whatever else one thinks of the metaphysics, this is intellectual integrity of a kind the field should want more of.

## 7. The strongest objections, and the best defense against each

A good apologist states the opposition at full strength. Here are the five objections a hostile specialist will bring, and the best available reply from the book's own resources.

**Objection 1 — The order-parameter account is a category error: it derives an *ought* from a *phase*.** *Broken symmetry is a physical state; that a ferromagnet's atoms are aligned entails nothing about what anyone should do. Calling moral facts order-parameters is a metaphor doing illicit normative work.*

*Reply.* The account is not deriving *ought* from the physics of magnets; the magnet is an analogy for the *logical structure* of a phase-relative fact that is veridical-within-phase and contingent-across-phase. The normativity does not come from the order-parameter analogy at all — it comes from the *co-constitutive floor* (§3), which is a separate argument grounded in the Null-Space Theorem, not in broken symmetry. The order-parameter account is doing *metaethical* work (explaining the two data of bindingness and variance), not *normative* work (telling you what to do). The book keeps these strictly separate: order-parameters explain the *phenomenology and variance* of moral facts; the co-constitutive floor supplies the *binding* invariants. The critic conflates the two chapters. When the book needs an *ought* (coercion is forbidden), it derives it from the theorem-plus-constituency, never from "that's how magnets behave." Read that way, no is/ought fallacy is committed, because the order-parameter account was never asked to bear normative weight — it bears explanatory weight, and the normativity enters through the constitutivist door.

**Objection 2 — The shmagent walks free after all (the deep version).** *Enoch's real point is that the constitutivist needs the norm to be one the agent cannot coherently reject* while still acting. *The book's egoist, on the "universalize" fork, coherently accepts the war of all against all — he does not want mastery, he is a nihilist who genuinely accepts mutual overridability. He is consistent and unrefuted.*

*Reply.* The book anticipates precisely this and answers it correctly: the consistent universalizer "purchases nothing he wanted... delivers not his mastery but the dimensionless point." The defense to press is that the objection smuggles in a *changed* adversary. Enoch's challenge to constitutivism is that the coercer can *retain his end* (getting what overriding gets him) while escaping the norm. The universalizer has *abandoned that end* — he no longer seeks a standing for his navigation that others lack; he accepts being overridden. But then he is not the egoist the objection began with; he is a being who has consented to his own dissolution, "the loop's corpse reached from the other side." The dialectic is: *either* you want asymmetric standing (exemption — closed by the theorem) *or* you accept symmetric overridability (universalization — which forfeits the end that motivated coercion and drains to the dimensionless point). There is no third position where you *both* retain the egoist's end *and* escape the norm. The shmagent's freedom was always parasitic on holding the end while dropping the commitment; the fork shows that in *this* framework — unlike Korsgaard's — dropping the commitment costs you the end, because the end (privileged standing) *is* the exemption the theorem forbids. That is why the co- matters: the standing is constituted relationally, so you cannot keep it while denying the relation.

The residual honest concession — the shrugger who accepts inconsistency — is *not* the shmagent. The shmagent claims to be *coherent*; the shrugger concedes incoherence and stops caring. Enoch's challenge is to the claim of *coherent* norm-evasion, and to *that* the book's answer holds: coherent evasion is not available. Incoherent evasion (the shrug) is available to everyone against every theory and is not what constitutivism was ever obliged to defeat.

**Objection 3 — "Auditability" is a thick, culturally parochial value masquerading as structure.** *The developmental audit-clause privileges a modern, Western, autonomy-fetishizing conception of the good child-rearing outcome. Traditional societies that form unquestioning members are indicted by a standard they never held.*

*Reply.* Defended in §2: the clause forbids *foreclosure* of the audit-capacity; it does not mandate that the capacity be exercised, nor prescribe any content the audit must reach. A tradition may form deeply committed, reverent, non-liberal members and still run *through* — so long as it leaves them *able*, in principle, to trace the provenance of what was installed and revise it. What it forbids is the *sealed* formation "built to remain unauditable and unrevisable by the mind it shaped." That is not the autonomy-fetish (which prizes actual critical distance and revision); it is the far weaker demand that the exit not be welded shut. Crucially, the demand is *derived*, not preferred: the Null-Space Theorem entails no aperture is self-transparent, so the capacity to audit is not held to an ideal of transparency (forbidden) but only to non-foreclosure. And the book pays the honest price: it concedes (Ubuntu card) that relational-traditional forms have a real tension with exit, and the audit-clause is its principled resolution — the thinnest cut that preserves the relational good while forbidding the sealed cage. A parochial value would indict all strong formation; this clause indicts only formation-built-to-prevent-its-own-review, which is a structurally specifiable and culturally portable wrong (the sealed cult vs. the mother tongue).

**Objection 4 — The whole edifice rests on a contested idealist metaphysics (the aware ground). If the metaphysics fails, the ethics falls.** *The Null-Space Theorem is only as good as the claim that a perspective-that-excludes-nothing is the undivided aware ground. A physicalist who denies the aware ground denies the theorem's premise 3.*

*Reply.* This is the most serious objection, and the honest defense is a *severability* argument the book itself licenses. The Null-Space Theorem, as stated in "The Arguments in Brief," does not actually require the *aware* ground — it requires only that (1) to be a perspective is to have content, (2) content is contrast, (3) a perspective excluding nothing would contrast with nothing and so have no content. Premises 1–3 are claims about *perspectives and content*, not about panpsychism. A physicalist who grants that perspectives are individuated by what they exclude — a wholly mainstream view in the epistemology of perspective and even in information theory (a channel is defined by what it cannot transmit) — grants the theorem. The idealist ground supplies the book's *explanation* of why there are perspectives at all and dissolves the hard problem, but the *ethics* needs only the theorem plus co-constitution, and both are statable in substrate-neutral terms. Indeed the book's own "one promise" at the census threshold ("The populated dark") makes the severability explicit for the ecology: "not one link of the later argument is lost" if you decline the contested metaphysics, because "the floor the next part builds needs only what any reader already grants: multi-scale navigators, the three orientations, and the difference between... *through*... and *over*." The ethics is deliberately built to be *portable off the metaphysics*. A referee who sinks the ethics by sinking the panpsychism has not read the severability clause the book installed precisely to survive that attack. (The book *should* foreground this more — see §8 — but the resource is there.)

**Objection 5 — The floor is so thin it is compatible with monstrous regimes, since almost everything is "phase-local weather."** *If content is relative and only the keel binds, then a society that keeps the exits formally open can still be grotesque, and the theory has no purchase.*

*Reply.* This underestimates what the keel forbids, once the ethics-of-attention corollaries are added. The floor forbids: coerced apertures, manufactured collapses, destruction of the relational carriers (malheur inflicted), *and* — via the diffuse-coercion clause — arrangements that leave "whole classes of navigator with structurally less room to move," which is "a violation *of* the floor." That last clause means the floor is *not* satisfied by formally-open exits if the arrangement systematically narrows some navigators' range — structural violence is a floor-violation, not permitted weather. Add the inequality-of-null-spaces result (the powerful can afford blindness the powerless cannot) and the ethics of attention's duty to attend against the groove, and the floor generates a substantial critique of exactly the monstrous-but-formally-open regime the objection imagines. The "weather" that is genuinely left open is content — what a good life *consists in*, which goods rank where — not the structural distribution of navigational freedom, which the floor governs. The book draws this line explicitly ("the content-relativity the floor leaves open... is genuine weather... But not everything that varies across positions is weather"). The keel is thin along the axis of *the good* and load-bearing along the axis of *coercion, erasure, and the distribution of freedom* — which is precisely the division a defensible thin ethics should draw.

## 8. What the book should do to make the ethics unassailable (constructive, in its own interest)

Placing the work in its best light includes telling the author where the strong position is under-defended, so that a hostile referee cannot land a blow the book has the resources to parry but does not.

1. **Foreground the severability of the ethics from the panpsychist metaphysics** (Objection 4). The resource exists — the census "one promise," and the substrate-neutral statement of the Null-Space Theorem in "The Arguments in Brief" — but it is scattered. A single paragraph in Part V stating "the floor requires only premises a physicalist grants: perspectives individuated by exclusion, agency constituted relationally" would immunize the ethics against the most damaging line of attack. As it stands, a referee reads Part I's idealism and assumes the ethics inherits its contestability. It does not, and the book should say so *where the ethics is argued*, not only where the census disclaims its menagerie.

2. **Name the constituency relation's own grounding more explicitly.** The claim "constituency is the primitive relation" is load-bearing against the shmagent (§3) but is asserted more than argued in Part V. The *argument* that agency is constituted-through-relation (not merely formed-in-relation — the modal difference matters, since "formed" is contingent and "constituted" is what the anti-exemption move needs) deserves a dedicated paragraph. The relational-ontology and developmental-psychology cards in the Coda (Vygotsky: "higher functions appear first *between* people and only then within") contain exactly the empirical support; pulling one sentence of it into the spine would convert an assertion into a derivation and close the seam Objection 2 tries to pry open.

3. **State the order-parameter/co-constitutive division of labor explicitly** (Objection 1). Because both live in Part V, a fast reader conflates them and hears "moral facts are physical phase-states, therefore ought-from-is." One sentence — "the order-parameter account explains the *phenomenology and variance* of moral facts; the *binding* comes not from broken symmetry but from the co-constitutive floor" — forecloses the category-error charge cleanly.

4. **Give "validity, not grip" its own defense as the correct sizing of the amoralist problem** (§3). The book states the concession beautifully but does not press the counterattack: *no* theory does better, and the demand for grip-by-proof is incoherent. Right now a referee can quote "validity, not grip" as an admission of defeat. Two sentences observing that this is the universal situation of ethics — and that the book's advance is to *build* grip through exits, community, and attention rather than pretend to prove it — turns the concession into a claimed advantage.

None of these is a repair to the argument; each is a *placement* of a resource the book already owns where the attack will land. The ethics is stronger than its current exposition reveals, which is the opposite of the usual problem.

## 9. Verdict

Read as a contribution to metaethics and normative ethics, *Perspective* does four things I have not seen done together, and two I have not seen done at all.

The four-together: it (i) reconciles realism's datum and relativism's datum in one mechanism (order-parameters) without positing queer facts or resorting to projection; (ii) grounds a genuinely universal floor without a summit, on structural invariants that *survive* Street's debunking precisely because they are structural; (iii) answers Enoch's shmagent where Korsgaard's solitary legislator cannot, by making agency *co*-constituted so that the egoist's privileged standing is self-undermining rather than merely inconsistent; and (iv) sizes the amoralist remainder correctly — validity secured, grip disclaimed and then addressed by practice rather than pretended-away by proof.

The two-unprecedented (to my knowledge): the **developmental audit-clause**, a temporal-retrospective reconstruction of autonomy that resolves the "shaped-before-consent" problem with a single meta-capability rather than a thick list, and applies to the making of computational minds; and the derivation of **inattention as erasure** from a metaphysics in which registration is participation — which supplies the *mechanism* that Murdoch, Weil, and Levinas could only assert, and yields the substantive, politically consequential result that null spaces are unequally distributed and that the comfortable are structurally blind to the harm their comfort rests on.

The through/over distinction is the instrument that makes all of this cut, and it cuts where thicker theories tangle: the two prisons, the discipline and the cage, the formation that hands over the key versus the one that welds the door. Its thinness is not a poverty but the source of its debunking-resistance and its cross-cultural portability. And the book's reflexive honesty — conceding the thinness and defending it, enforcing the provenance test symmetrically on reformer and fanatic alike, holding the malheur boundary and the altitude rule against its own most sweeping sentences, and convicting its own conditions of production under its own criterion — is not a stockpile of admissions but the mark of a theory that means what it says.

The most serious vulnerability is the ethics' apparent dependence on a contested idealist ground, and the best defense is that the dependence is only apparent: the floor is statable, and the book itself states it, in substrate-neutral terms. The book should make that severability loud. Do that, and this is the most complete answer I know to the question the field has been unable to answer since Mackie — how a thin, structurally grounded ethics can bind everywhere without a shelf in the universe to write the binding on. It binds from everywhere that navigates, because it concerns what navigating is. That is the right answer, and *Perspective* has earned it.
