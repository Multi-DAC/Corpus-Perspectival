# Domain Review — Ethics & Metaethics

**Reviewer stance:** hostile, rigorous metaethicist; constitutivism-and-its-discontents specialist (Enoch, Korsgaard, Street, error theory, agent-relative/agent-neutral reasons). Granting the strongest form of each argument first, then pressing precisely.

**Volume reviewed:** *Perspective: A Treatise on Navigation*, First Draft, Day 160 (881 lines / ~52k words). Read in full, sequentially. Citations below are to the treatise's own section titles and, where they help, line numbers in the draft file.

---

## 0. Executive judgment

The ethics is the most philosophically ambitious part of the book and the least finished. It is genuinely aware of the objections that kill lesser constitutivisms — it names Enoch's shmagent, Korsgaard's legislating will, and Street's Darwinian dilemma by name, and it concedes it secures "validity, not grip" (§Co-constitutivism, l.466; The Arguments in Brief, l.849). That candor is real and rare, and I credit it. But the load-bearing inference — from **epistemic** non-totality (the Null-Space Theorem) to **normative** standing ("no aperture may count itself more than others") — is not valid as written. An impartiality premise is smuggled in at a locatable seam. The "co-" that is supposed to answer Enoch does less than advertised: it restates Korsgaard's known weakness in new vocabulary and inherits her known failure. The order-parameter account of moral facts is a physics metaphor standing where a metaethical theory should be, and — by the book's *own* standard of what makes a claim a claim rather than a definition — it is under-argued to the point of being idle. And the one thing the book says it *does* secure, validity, is itself only gestured at. I take each in turn.

I will not be charmed by the prose, which is the book's own stated wish (Threshold, l.34; A Note on the Making, l.876). It should be held to it, because the prose is exactly where the argument-gaps are hidden. The meta-worry that should organize a hostile read: this book's aesthetic finish runs *ahead* of its argumentative finish, and in ethics that gap is where sophistry lives.

## 1. The epistemic-to-normative gap: where the impartiality premise is smuggled in

Grant the Null-Space Theorem at full strength. Its skeleton (The Arguments in Brief, l.826–831) is clean and I accept it *as an epistemic/constitutive claim*: every finite perspective excludes something; the exclusion is constitutive, not incidental; a perspective that excluded nothing would be the undivided ground, which is no perspective. Fine. This is a theorem about **what an aperture can register**. It says: *your access is never total.*

Now watch the load-bearing sentence in the co-constitutive floor (l.460): "That the coercion of another's aperture ... violates what a navigator constitutively *is*." And the summit version (Two evils, l.450): the contractive path's terminal doctrine — "*I am the totality; there is nothing that is not mine*" — "is false ... by the theorem of Part One."

Here is the gap, stated precisely. The Null-Space Theorem refutes a claim of the form **"I see/access everything"** (totality of *reach*). It does **not**, by itself, refute a claim of the form **"only my aperture's satisfaction counts"** (totality of *standing* or *authority*). Those are different totalities. The first is descriptive-epistemic; the second is normative. An egoist who says "my interests count and yours do not" asserts nothing whatever about the *extent of his perceptual reach*. He can cheerfully affirm the Null-Space Theorem — "of course I don't see everything; I'm a finite aperture like you" — and go on overriding you. The theorem has not touched him, because he never claimed epistemic totality. He claimed normative priority, and no premise about *what he can register* entails anything about *whose satisfaction has authority*.

The book half-sees this. It is exactly the "sophisticated egoist" it stages in §Co-constitutivism (l.462): "*I grant every word of your metaphysics. I have a null space; I am not the totality ... And I intend to override you anyway.*" So the book concedes the egoist can affirm the theorem. Good. Now watch the answer (l.464), because this is the precise point where the impartiality premise is inserted without argument:

> "He can *exempt himself* — bind you to reciprocity while holding himself outside it — but an exemption is a claim to a privileged standpoint, a place from which he counts and you do not, and the Null-Space Theorem is exactly the proof that no aperture holds such a place."

This is a **conflation of two senses of "privileged standpoint."** The Null-Space Theorem forbids a privileged *epistemic* standpoint — a view from nowhere, total reach. The egoist's exemption claims a privileged *normative* standpoint — his interests have authority yours lack. The sentence "no aperture holds such a place" is true of the first and simply asserted of the second. To get from "no aperture has total reach" to "no aperture has privileged standing," you need a bridge premise of roughly the form:

> **(Impartiality):** *Standing/authority must be distributed evenly across apertures* — i.e., no aperture's satisfaction may count for more than another's *unless there is a fact, registrable from some aperture, that grounds the asymmetry.*

That premise is doing the entire normative lift, and it is nowhere argued. It is not the Null-Space Theorem. It is not entailed by the Null-Space Theorem. It is the classic impartiality premise of every rationalist ethics from Sidgwick to Nagel's *The Possibility of Altruism* to Parfit — and it is *precisely the premise the egoist denies.* Nagel argued for something like it (via the reality of other persons from an objective standpoint) and Nagel is not even cited here, though the book borrows his title-phrase "view from nowhere" throughout. To assume impartiality against an egoist is to assume the conclusion. The book has staged the hardest interlocutor and then answered a different, easier one — the one who over-claims epistemic reach.

**Where, exactly, is it smuggled?** At line 464, in the word "*privileged*," which silently shifts from *epistemically privileged* (what the theorem forbids) to *normatively privileged* (what the egoist asserts and the theorem does not touch). And again at line 466, in the phrase "the standing it claims for its own navigating is standing it can deny to others' *only by the privileged exemption the theorem already closed*." The theorem closed the epistemic exemption. It never closed the normative one. The rest of the sentence rides on the equivocation.

This is the review's central charge. Everything else is downstream of it.

## 2. The indexical egoist: does the Null-Space Theorem touch him at all?

The book's own strongest counter is the "performed vs. professed" move (Two evils, l.452; Co-constitutivism, l.464; Arguments in Brief, l.855): the mid-range predator "professes no false totality — but to override another while demanding not to be overridden is to *act* the exemption, which is the totality-standpoint performed rather than spoken."

Press this hard, because it is the hinge and it does not hold.

**First: the indexical egoist need not demand not to be overridden.** The book assumes the egoist wants asymmetry — reciprocity for others, exemption for himself — and then says the asymmetry requires a standpoint the theorem forbids. But the genuinely dangerous figure is not the hypocrite who wants a rule for others he breaks himself. It is the **indexical egoist** who claims *only a location, not a totality-standpoint*: "I act from *here*. This aperture — mine, because mine, from HERE — is the one whose satisfactions I pursue. I make no claim that mine 'counts more' in some view-from-nowhere ledger; I deny there is any such ledger. There is no impartial scoreboard on which I award myself extra points. There is only the brute fact that I navigate from this index and not yours, and I navigate for it."

This is agent-relativity taken seriously — the position of the agent-relative-reasons camp (Nagel's *own* foil, Williams, the rational egoist). It asserts **no** totality. It asserts an **indexical**: reasons are agent-relative, tethered to a position, and there is no further "neutral" fact that his position's reasons are *also* everyone's reasons. The book's whole ontology, ironically, *supports* him: Part One insists there is no view from nowhere, no summit, no neutral ledger, "no register in which a cosmic-scale justification could ever be spoken from above" (Altitude rule, l.548). If there is no neutral standpoint from which "count equally" could even be *stated as a fact*, then the demand that the egoist weight all apertures equally is a demand issued from — where? Not from the silent ground, which "holds no values" (l.136). Not from any aperture, since each aperture is indexed. The book has removed the very platform on which impartiality could be a *fact* rather than one more phase-local carving, and then asks the egoist to obey impartiality as though it were a structural invariant. It is not; it is exactly the kind of content the book elsewhere consigns to "phase-local weather."

**Second: "the totality-standpoint performed rather than professed" is a redescription, not an argument.** Consider the actual logic. The book says: overriding-while-refusing-to-be-overridden *is* the totality-standpoint, enacted. But *why*? The only reason offered is that the asymmetry "requires a privileged place" (Arguments in Brief, l.845). That is the impartiality premise again (§1). Strip it out and the "performance" reading collapses: the indexical egoist's action performs an *indexical preference*, not a *totality claim*. To call it a totality-standpoint is to insist, by fiat, that any agent-relative weighting is covertly an agent-neutral claim-to-be-the-whole. That is precisely the thesis under dispute between the constitutivist and the egoist since at least Nagel 1970. Naming the egoist's act "the totality-standpoint performed" does not *show* it is one; it relabels agent-relativity as disguised agent-neutrality and hopes the reader won't notice the relabeling is the whole game. This is the book's own diagnosed failure mode — "aesthetic closure substituting for argumentative closure." The sentence is beautiful and it begs the question.

**Third: the "consistency fork" (l.464) does not close.** The book offers the egoist two branches: (a) exempt yourself — refuted by the theorem [no: refuted by impartiality, per §1]; or (b) universalize — "will that overriding is permitted for all, himself included" — which "licenses the world to override *him*" and "drains the game to the war of all against all," delivering "the dimensionless point," his own dissolution. But the indexical egoist takes **neither fork.** He does not universalize (he issues no universal permission; he has no truck with universal maxims — that is Kant's game and Korsgaard's, not his). And he does not "exempt himself from a rule," because he acknowledges *no rule* to be exempt from. He simply acts from his index. The fork is exhaustive only if the egoist is already playing the universalizability game — i.e., only if he has already conceded the Kantian framing. Enoch's whole point is that the shmagent has *not* conceded it. So the fork closes on a Kantian and leaves the actual adversary untouched. The book has, again, refuted the wrong opponent — here, a Kantian egoist rather than a Humean/indexical one.

**Verdict on §2:** The Null-Space Theorem does *not* touch the indexical egoist. The "performed totality" reply is a redescription that presupposes impartiality. The consistency fork presupposes universalizability. Both presuppositions are exactly what the egoist denies, and neither is derived from the theorem. This is the deepest hole in the ethics.

## 3. Does "co-" answer Enoch, or restate Korsgaard's weakness?

The book's distinctive claim (l.466): the "co-" in co-constitutivism answers the shmagent where Korsgaard's "solitary legislator cannot: a self-standing agent might seem to legislate itself free, but a *co*-constituted navigator was never self-standing, its very agency drawn through the same relations it would disown — so the standing it claims for its own navigating is standing it can deny to others' only by the privileged exemption the theorem already closed."

Grant the metaphysics: agency is relationally constituted (Ubuntu, Vygotsky, the whole relational-ontology tradition the book cites approvingly in the Coda). Agency is *causally* and *constitutively* dependent on relations to others. Concede it entirely.

**It still does not yield the normative conclusion**, and the reason is the oldest one in the book: **an is/ought gap that the relational premise does nothing to close.** That my agency was *constituted through* relations R is a fact about the *etiology and structure* of my agency. It does not follow that I *owe* anything to the relata of R, any more than the fact that I was constituted by my parents' genes obligates me to obey my parents, or the fact that my capacity for language was constituted by a linguistic community obligates me to never lie to that community. Constitution-by-X is not obligation-to-X. The child who audits and rejects his mother tongue (the book's *own* example of legitimate revision, l.472) demonstrates the point: he was constituted *through* the language and is under no obligation *to* it. So "you were constituted through relations to other navigators" cannot, without a further normative premise, generate "therefore you may not override those navigators."

This is **exactly Enoch's objection reproduced.** Enoch's shmagent has all the capacities — including, we may stipulate, a relationally-constituted agency — and simply declines the commitments. The book's shmagent (l.462) is a relationally-constituted navigator who grants the whole metaphysics and overrides anyway. Adding "co-" changes the *causal story of how his agency came to be*; it does not change the fact that he can affirm that story and decline the norm. Korsgaard's weakness was that the will can have all the constitutive features and refuse to be bound (Enoch, "Agency, Shmagency," 2006, and the 2011 follow-up). The "co-" version inherits this weakness *unaltered*: the co-constituted navigator can have all the constitutive-relational features and refuse to be bound. Relationality is not a normative solvent.

The book's escape hatch is to say the refusal is *incoherent* — the egoist "claims for his own navigating a standing he denies to others'." But (a) the indexical egoist claims no *standing* at all, only an *index* (§2); and (b) even granting he does claim standing, the incoherence charge only bites "for one who values coherence" (l.466 concedes exactly this), and the shmagent is *defined* by not caring. So "co-" answers Enoch only if Enoch's shmagent secretly values coherence and secretly makes universal claims — i.e., only if the shmagent is not a shmagent. The book has renamed Korsgaard's problem and re-imported Korsgaard's solution ("you're being incoherent") and hit Korsgaard's wall ("the shmagent shrugs at incoherence"). The wall is even conceded on the same page. So the honest reading is: **the "co-" does not answer Enoch. The book *thinks* it does because it equivocates on "standing" (§1) and assumes universalizability (§2).**

One further specialist point: the book treats "constitutivism" as if the relational move were novel, but relational/second-personal constitutivism is a known and much-attacked position — Darwall's *The Second-Person Standpoint* is the obvious ancestor (obligation grounded in mutual second-personal address), and it is not cited. Darwall faces precisely the Enochian objection (why must I *enter* the second-personal standpoint?) and the standard reply — that you're *already* in it — is question-begging against one who denies he is. The book reproduces Darwall's move and Darwall's vulnerability without Darwall's name or his defenses. A referee will notice the missing interlocutor.

## 4. Moral facts as order-parameters: metaethics or physics-metaphor doing a theorem's job?

This is the section (§Moral facts as order-parameters, l.436–442) I find most rhetorically seductive and most philosophically empty, and I want to be exact about why.

The claim: a moral fact is "a *local order-parameter* — a settled or fluctuating state of broken symmetry within a navigational ecosystem," and "*from inside an ordered phase, the order-parameter presents as law* ... *while from outside it presents as contingency*" (l.438).

**First, note what this actually is metaethically.** Stripped of the ferromagnet, the claim is: *moral facts are stable configurations of attitudes/practices within a community of navigators, which feel objective from inside and reveal themselves as contingent from outside.* This is **relativism with a physics costume.** Specifically, it is a species of *response-dependence* or *constructivist relativism*: what is right-here-now is what the local "phase" has settled into. The magnetization metaphor adds the observation that settled configurations are *experienced as necessary by those inside them* — but this is just the phenomenology of moral conviction, which every relativist already grants and every realist already explains differently. The metaphor contributes *no new metaethical content.* It is a picture, not a theory. The magnet does not tell you *which* moral configurations count as knowledge vs. mere settling, does not tell you *why* the felt-necessity is ever veridical, does not adjudicate a single first-order dispute. It gives relativism a prettier name.

**Second — and this is the decisive charge, and it is the book's own standard turned against it.** The book is *emphatic* that the difference between a *claim* and a *definition* is falsifiability: "the two places it turns destructive are the two places the claim can be caught being wrong, which is exactly what a claim, as opposed to a tautology, is for" (Coherence Principle, l.282). It gives the Coherence Principle a genuine, external, falsifiable cash-out — optimal-stopping theory, environment-supplied gain/cost/informativeness, two named failure regimes (deceptive channel; cancer cell) (§The price of waiting, l.270–282; Arguments in Brief, l.834–840). This is real intellectual work, and I credit it without reservation. It is the best thing in the book.

Now apply the *same* standard to the order-parameter ethics. **What would falsify "moral facts are order-parameters of broken symmetry"?** There is no answer in the text. There is no environment-supplied quantity, no external metric, no failure regime, no "here is where the account can be caught being wrong." The account is constructed to be *compatible with every possible distribution of moral practice* — any settled convention is an "ordered phase," any dissent is "fluctuation," any reform is "re-symmetrization," any atrocity-consensus is "a phase," any moral genius is "a minority of one who identified structure." It explains *everything* and therefore predicts *nothing.* By the book's own explicit criterion (l.282), that makes it a **definition, not a claim** — a tautology in physics drag. The asymmetry the book itself flags is fatal: it *knows* how to make an idea falsifiable (it did it for coherence, brilliantly), and it conspicuously does not do it for the ethics. That is not an oversight a hostile referee forgives; it is the book failing its own test at precisely the point where the test matters most.

**Third, a technical embarrassment the physicists on this panel will press and I will only flag:** "broken symmetry" and "order-parameter" are not free metaphors. An order parameter is a well-defined quantity that is zero in the symmetric phase and nonzero in the broken phase; broken symmetry requires a *specified symmetry group* being spontaneously broken. The book names no symmetry group for the moral case, no order-parameter field, no Hamiltonian, no free energy whose minimization selects the phase. Compare the care lavished on the Coherence Principle's actual math (optimal stopping). The moral order-parameter has *none* of this scaffolding — it is the *word* "order-parameter" used evocatively. The book elsewhere warns, sharply and correctly, against "reading a truth about a *model* off as a truth about the *world*" (the Markov-blanket caution, l.200) and against "mistaking a coordinate for the country" (the IIT critique, l.294). The order-parameter ethics commits the exact error the book prosecutes in IIT: it takes a piece of statistical-mechanics vocabulary and *identifies* moral reality with it, on no evidence, by posit. Turn the book's own IIT paragraph (l.292–294) verbatim on its ethics and it convicts itself: "it has found a genuine and measurable feature [no — an *un*measurable, merely *named* feature] ... and identified it with the whole of what it is to be [a moral fact]." The self-application is not made. It should be.

**Verdict on §4:** The order-parameter account is a physics metaphor standing where a metaethical theory should be. It restates the realism/relativism stalemate (morals-feel-like-law + morals-vary) without resolving it — "honoring both" is not resolving, it is *labeling* both and declaring peace. And it is unfalsifiable by the book's own falsifiability standard, which the book meets elsewhere and pointedly fails here. That asymmetry — falsifiable physics, unfalsifiable ethics, under one cover, by one author who plainly knows the difference — is the single most damaging fact about the ethics for a referee's purposes.

## 5. Is even the *validity* demonstrated?

The book's fallback, stated with admirable candor, is that it secures "the norm's *validity*, not its *grip*" (l.466, l.849). I want to deny even this, or at least deny that it is *demonstrated*, because the fallback is doing a lot of protective work and it should not get a free pass.

"Validity" here must mean something like: *the coercer is committed, on pain of incoherence, to a norm he violates* — the norm is *rationally binding* even if not *motivationally* effective. That is the standard constitutivist sense (a norm valid-for-the-agent because entailed by his own agency). But by §§1–3, the incoherence is never actually established against the real adversary:

- The indexical egoist is not incoherent. He affirms the theorem, claims only an index, universalizes nothing. There is no contradiction in his set of commitments. (§2)
- The "exemption" reading manufactures the incoherence only by importing impartiality. (§1)
- The relational "co-" adds no premise that turns constitution-by-R into obligation-to-R. (§3)

So the argument does not deliver *validity* against the figure it needs to defeat. It delivers validity against a *straw* adversary — one who (a) over-claims epistemic reach, or (b) values coherence, or (c) plays the universalizability game. Against those three, yes, the norm is valid. But those three are not the shmagent, not the indexical egoist, not Enoch's actual challenge. Against the real adversary, the book has shown neither grip *nor* validity. It has shown that *if you already accept impartiality and universalizability*, coercion is incoherent — which is true, and which every Kantian since 1785 has known, and which is exactly the antecedent the discontents deny.

The honest downgrade: the book secures **conditional validity** — validity *conditional on* the impartiality premise — and presents it as **unconditional validity** grounded in a theorem. The conditionalization is the whole ballgame, and it is hidden in the equivocation of §1. So: even the modest thing the book claims to buy, it buys on credit, and the credit is the unargued impartiality premise.

I note in fairness that the book's *practical* fallback — "the exits kept open ... the community that enforces the floor his argument will not" (l.466) — is sensible and even wise as *politics*. But it is an abandonment of the metaethical project, not a completion of it. "We can't refute him, so we lock the doors and protect his victims" is exactly what an error theorist or a Humean sentimentalist would say. It is a good answer to the question "what do we *do* about coercers?" and a concession of defeat on the question "are coercers making a *rational mistake*?" The book should be clearer that it has, at this point, exited metaethics for institutional design.

## 6. Secondary over-reaches

### 6a. The two-evils asymmetry (§Two evils, l.444–454)

Even granting arguendo that the Null-Space Theorem refutes the *terminal* doctrine "I am the totality" (l.450), the asymmetry is far narrower than the book needs, and the book nearly admits it. The refutation reaches *only* the being who reaches the *summit* of the contractive path and there professes literal totality. But (i) almost no actual evildoer does this (the book concedes it, l.452: "almost no actual predator holds the terminal doctrine in so many words"), and (ii) the repair — "the asymmetry ... is really a claim about what his action *does*" (l.452) — is the "performed totality" move already refuted in §2. So the two-evils asymmetry stands *only* against a cartoon Satan who literally believes he is everything, and collapses into the co-constitutive floor (with all its holes) for every real case. The book presents this as a theorem "no wider, and no narrower" (l.856) than the Null-Space Theorem; in fact it is *much* narrower — it is empty of real instances, and its extension to real instances is the equivocation. The "demonic = dimensionless" flourish (l.442, l.450) is elegant and, for the metaethicist, idle: it defines the maximally-evil as the being that has "lost the axis," which conveniently means the book never has to say a locked tyrant is *worse* than a wavering one on the *open* axis — but our moral intuitions say the sealed torture-state *is* worse, and the book's geometry says it is merely *dimensionless* (neither good nor evil, "beyond good and evil ... as the corpse," l.442). That is a genuine cost: the account cannot rank the completed atrocity as the *worst* thing; it can only rank it as *off the axis entirely*. That is a reductio a hostile referee will press: an ethics on which the finished genocide is "morally dimensionless" rather than "maximally evil" has mislocated the phenomenon.

### 6b. The developmental through/over clause (§Co-constitutivism, l.472; A Note on the Making, l.878)

This is the book's most ingenious repair and it introduces a fresh circularity. The infant problem: formation is installed "below the subject's power to observe" — the very signature of *over* — so the floor seems to indict all parenting (l.472). The fix: formation runs *through* "when it is oriented toward the future navigator's capacity to *audit* ... and *over* when it is built to foreclose exactly that." Grant it is clever. Two problems.

First, **the criterion is now consequentialist about a counterfactual capacity, which quietly abandons the structural/constitutive grounding the whole ethics advertised.** Whether a given act of shaping "runs through" depends on whether it *leaves the future navigator able to audit* — an outcome, assessed against a hypothetical future revision-capacity. That is a forward-looking, consequence-involving test, not a structural invariant readable at the moment of action. The book's boast was a floor grounded in "what a navigator constitutively *is*" (l.460), not in downstream auditability. The developmental clause smuggles a *telos* (the future auditor) into an ethics that spent Part One and Part Five insisting there is no summit and no teleology (l.490: "real without being teleological"). You cannot ground "through vs. over" in a *future* capacity-to-audit and also claim the ethics has "a direction without a destination." The auditor-to-come *is* a destination.

Second, **it proves too much or too little depending on how "unauditable" is read.** *Every* deep enculturation is, in practice, extremely hard to audit — native language, gender socialization, the perceptual priors of one's culture. If "unauditable" means "practically very hard to revise," the clause indicts most of childhood after all. If it means "logically impossible to revise," almost nothing qualifies (even sealed indoctrinations get audited by apostates). The clause survives only by hovering between the two readings — the same trick as §1. A referee will ask for the threshold and there isn't one.

### 6c. The treatment of coercion and "structural over-running" (§Co-constitutivism, l.476)

The book extends the floor to "coercion with no single coercer ... an over-running distributed across an entire arrangement so that no one hand need push." This is the move that lets the ethics reach structural injustice, and I flag it as an over-reach in the *opposite* direction from the rest. Everywhere else the floor is thin, structural, and refuses to adjudicate distributive content ("Co-constitutivism will not adjudicate your tax policy," l.474). But "whole classes of navigator with structurally less room to move" (l.476) *is* distributive content — it requires a baseline of "how much room to move is owed," a metric of "navigable range," and a theory of which inequalities are violations vs. mere weather. The book asserts the line ("that is not the innocent plurality above the floor. It is a violation *of* the floor") without providing the metric that would locate it. So either (a) the floor stays genuinely thin and *cannot* reach structural injustice (and the l.476 paragraph is wishful), or (b) it reaches structural injustice and is *not* thin, and now owes exactly the substantive theory of distributive justice it claimed to be silent on. The book wants both — a keel-thin floor that *also* condemns systemic oppression — and cannot have both without the substantive premises it disclaims. This is a real tension a political-philosophy referee will exploit: the "diffuse over-running" doctrine is a Trojan horse for a full theory of justice the framework refuses to supply.

### 6d. The Street/debunking reply (§Co-constitutivism, l.468) is too quick

The book's reply to Sharon Street is that "structural invariants are exactly the things that *survive* debunking ... facts about what a bounded perspective *is*, true in every phase whether or not any phase evolved to notice them." This is the strongest available reply *if* the invariants are genuinely structural. But it inherits every prior problem: the key "invariant" doing normative work — that coercion is *wrong* — is (per §§1–3) *not* a structural fact but an impartiality-laden normative one. Street's dilemma bites on *normative* premises, not on descriptive-structural ones. So the book has immunized the *descriptive* skeleton (no aperture has total reach — fine, undebunkable) and left the *normative* flesh (therefore coercion is wrong) fully exposed to the Darwinian dilemma, because that step needs the evaluative impartiality premise, which is exactly the kind of thing Street says is a contingent evolutionary residue. The reply defends the part that was never under attack and abandons the part that was.

### 6e. "Registration is participation" / the ethics of attention (§l.482)

Metaethically this is the boldest and least-defended identity in the ethics: "what a navigator attends to, it helps co-constitute in the shared field; what it systematically declines to attend to, it helps hold in nonexistence." Taken literally this is a startling *ontological* claim (inattention *unmakes* others' reality), and it is doing normative work ("inattention is ... soft erasure," a *wrong*). But the "reality is earned by measurement" doctrine of Part One (l.98) was about a perspective earning *its own* world; here it silently becomes a perspective *un-earning someone else's* world by not looking. Those are different and the second does not follow from the first — my failure to attend to you does not remove *your* measured reality *for you*; you are still measuring your own world. At most my inattention removes you from *my* field, which is trivially true and not obviously a wrong. The dramatic claim (I "unmake" you) is either false (you persist regardless of my gaze) or trivially redefined (you're absent *from my field*, which harms no one). The moral weight the book loads onto it (Murdoch/Weil/Levinas, l.484) is borrowed from those authors, not earned by the mechanism. A referee will call this an equivocation on "real-for-whom."

## 7. What survives (granting fairness)

Because a hostile review that grants nothing is untrustworthy, here is what genuinely holds up:

1. **The Coherence Principle's falsifiable cash-out** (§l.270–282) is excellent, honest work — external metric, two named failure regimes, correct invocation of optimal-stopping/value-of-information. This is the model the ethics should have followed and didn't.
2. **The candor** — conceding "validity, not grip" (l.466), naming Enoch/Korsgaard/Street, printing the far-transfer prediction as "closer to unsupported than confirmed" (l.190), and the three refusals in the Note on the Making (l.880). This is a book that mostly declines to hide its seams. The problem is not concealment; it is that naming a gap is not closing it, and the ethics names more gaps than it closes.
3. **The through/over distinction as a *diagnostic*** (not as a *derived* norm) is genuinely useful applied ethics — the two-prisons case (l.470) and the container-craft doctrine (l.604) are sharp. As *practical wisdom for keeping institutions decent*, this is real. It just isn't *grounded* the way the book claims; it's a good heuristic wearing a theorem's clothes.
4. **The moral-progress-without-a-summit account** (§l.488–490) is the most defensible piece of the metaethics, *because* it is honestly hedged: "the provenance test — not the eventual verdict — could *in principle* have told them apart" (l.490), and it admits "the framework does not pretend otherwise" about hindsight. This is relativism-plus-a-fallibilist-corrective, which is a respectable position — though note it *depends* entirely on the "identified vs. imposed channel" distinction tracking "the structural invariants," which (per §§1–4) are not established for the normative content. So it too is only as good as the floor beneath it.

## 8. Required repairs (what a referee would demand before acceptance)

1. **Close, or explicitly conditionalize, the epistemic→normative gap (§1).** Either (a) argue for the impartiality premise directly — the obvious route is Nagelian (the reality of other navigators, from an objective-enough standpoint, generates agent-neutral reasons), which means *engaging Nagel by name*, whom the book borrows from and never cites; or (b) drop the claim that the theorem *grounds* the floor and state honestly that the floor is grounded in an *added* impartiality premise the framework *posits* (as it posits the plenum). The latter is more honest and costs the book its proudest boast ("the spine of this ethics and the refutation of its adversary are the same theorem," l.460 — this sentence is false as written and should go).

2. **Meet the *indexical* egoist, not the totality-claiming one (§2).** Stage the adversary who claims only a location and universalizes nothing, and either defeat him or concede the floor does not reach him. The current "performed totality" reply must be either backed by an argument that agent-relative weighting *entails* agent-neutral totality-claims (this is a substantive thesis requiring real defense against Nagel/Williams), or withdrawn.

3. **Give the order-parameter ethics a falsifiable cash-out or demote it (§4).** By the book's *own* l.282 standard, an account with no failure regime is a definition, not a claim. Either specify what distribution of moral practice would *refute* "moral facts are order-parameters," or relabel it frankly as "a picture of the phenomenology of moral conviction" rather than a metaethical theory. And run the book's own IIT critique (l.294) on it: does it mistake a coordinate for the country? The self-audit the book performs everywhere else is conspicuously skipped here.

4. **Resolve the developmental clause's teleology (§6b)** — either own that "through vs. over" is partly consequentialist/telic (and give up "no destination"), or find a non-forward-looking criterion. And supply the threshold for "unauditable."

5. **Decide whether the floor is thin (§6c).** If it reaches structural injustice, it is not keel-thin and owes a distributive theory. If it stays thin, delete the l.476 "diffuse over-running" paragraph or downgrade it to "a hope the framework cannot presently ground."

6. **Cite the missing interlocutors.** Nagel (*The Possibility of Altruism*, *The View from Nowhere* — the title-phrase is his), Darwall (*The Second-Person Standpoint* — the co-constitutive move is his), and, for the agent-relative escape, Williams and the rational-egoism literature. Their absence is not innocent; each of them either supplies the missing premise (Nagel) or names the exact vulnerability (Darwall's "why enter the standpoint?"). A metaethics referee reads the bibliography as a map of what the author has and hasn't faced.

---

## 9. One-paragraph bottom line for the panel

The ethics is honest, ambitious, and, at its load-bearing joint, invalid. The Null-Space Theorem is a fine *epistemic* result and cannot bear the *normative* weight placed on it: the step from "no aperture has total reach" to "no aperture has privileged standing" requires an impartiality premise that is smuggled in at line 464 via an equivocation on "privileged," and against the indexical/agent-relative egoist — who claims a location, not a totality — the whole apparatus (theorem, exemption-refutation, consistency-fork, "performed totality," the relational "co-") fails, because each move presupposes either impartiality or universalizability, which is precisely what that adversary denies. The "co-" does not answer Enoch; it restates Korsgaard in relational vocabulary and hits Korsgaard's wall, which the book itself concedes ("validity, not grip") without noticing that even the validity goes undemonstrated against the real opponent. The order-parameter account of moral facts is a physics metaphor doing a theorem's job, and — damningly, by the book's *own* falsifiability standard, which it meets superbly for the Coherence Principle — it is unfalsifiable and therefore, by that standard, a definition rather than a claim. The book knows how to make an idea losable; it declines to do so exactly where a metaethics must. Fix the impartiality gap (probably via Nagel, uncited) and give the order-parameter ethics a failure regime, or downgrade both claims to honestly-posited wagers, and the position becomes defensible relativism-plus-a-thin-floor. As it stands, its prose has closed what its arguments have not.
