# THE INSIDE VIEW — Chapter Seven

## The Only Moment There Is

*Movement Two — what changes.*

Try something simple, right now: experience a moment other than this one.

You can't. Not because you aren't trying hard enough — because there is nowhere else to go. You can remember this morning, and the remembering happens *now*. You can picture tomorrow, and the picturing happens *now*. You have spent your entire life — every second of it, without a single exception — at exactly one address. Here. The present edge of your own experience.

This is so obvious it sounds like nothing. Of course you're here now; where else would you be? But hold it a beat longer, because underneath the obviousness is something the first half of this book has quietly made unavoidable, and it changes how a life feels from the inside.

We treat time like a place you can stand. The past is "back there," the future "up ahead," and the present a thin moving line between two vast countries you could, in principle, visit. But you can't visit them. You never have. The past is not a place — it is a *representation*, a model your brain builds and renders, right now, out of present material. The future is the same: a simulation, running now. When you "go back" to a memory, you do not leave; you stay exactly where you are and watch a present picture. When you "look ahead," you have not moved an inch.

So there are not three times you can occupy. There is one — and two kinds of present representation, one tagged *past* and one tagged *future*, that feel like elsewhere but are made entirely of here. The now is not the thin line between the two countries. The now is the only country. The other two are postcards mailed from it.

Now — you have heard a version of this before, and you have every right to be suspicious. *Be present. The now is all there is.* It comes printed over a photograph of a sunrise, and it usually arrives as an instruction with no reason attached: a thing you are supposed to *do*, by an effort you can never quite sustain, toward a state that always seems to be slipping away. If that is what you think I'm selling, you should brace yourself the way you did in Chapter Six.

But this book owes you a reason, and Movement One already paid it. Remember what we found: experience is not *produced* by the machinery — it is the ground the machinery runs on, and it happens, always, as a present act of gathering. The texture of a *now* was the entire subject of Chapter Five. If that is right, then "the present moment" is not a special state you reach by quieting your mind on a cushion. It is simply *where experience is.* The only place it has ever been. You are not being asked to *get* to the present. You are being shown that you have never once left it, and could not if you tried.

That is the whole difference between an instruction and a fact. "Be present" is exhausting because it sounds like an accomplishment to win against resistance, again and again, forever. *You are present — you always have been, you cannot be otherwise* is not an accomplishment. It is a description. And descriptions do not have to be sustained. They are just true, whether or not you are looking.

So if you cannot actually leave the now, why does so much of life feel like being somewhere else?

Because the postcards are vivid. The mind is a tireless renderer of the two absent countries, and it is very good at making them feel like the real terrain. Most of what we call suffering — not the sharp kind, the body's honest pain, but the long ache underneath it — is built from present representations of absent things. Regret is a present picture, tagged *past*, that you mistake for a place you still live in. Anxiety is a present simulation, tagged *future*, that you mistake for a thing already happening to you. You can be sitting in a quiet, warm, perfectly safe room — and suffering — because the rendering is so convincing you have forgotten it is a rendering.

I am not going to tell you to stop thinking about the past or the future. That is both impossible and unwise; the renderer is how you learn and plan and love people who aren't in the room, and a self that could not model time would be a poorer self, not a freer one. The shift is not to silence the maps. It is to feel, under the vivid postcard of the dreaded tomorrow, the texture of the actual room — which is, nearly every time you check, *fine*. The territory is almost always more bearable than the postcard of it. The whole move, if there is one, is just remembering to look up from the picture to the place you are actually standing.

And here is the part I find genuinely consoling — the part the rigor pays for.

You cannot fall out of the present. You have never missed your life. That very worry — that you are not living enough in the moment, that it is all rushing past while you're distracted — is itself only another postcard, a present picture tagged *regret-about-time*. But the now does not require your attention in order to hold you. Recall the seamlessness from Chapter Five: you never experience your own gaps. Whatever happens in your stream, you are *by definition* at its present edge — there is no stranded version of you back in the past, no version waiting in the future wondering where you are. There is only ever the one of you, here, at the leading edge of the gathering, exactly where you have always been.

This is not a discipline to maintain or a state to earn. It is a homecoming to a house you never actually left — you had only been staring so hard at the postcards on the wall that you forgot you were standing in the very room they were sent from. You do not have to *become* present. You have to notice that you already are, and that there was never a door.

So that is the first thing that changes when you stop treating the inside view as a problem to be explained away and start treating it as the ground you stand on: time loosens its grip. Not because you have mastered anything — because you have seen where you actually live, and it turns out to be the one place that was never going anywhere.

There is a second thing, and it follows from the first, though it travels somewhere you might not expect. Because once you are standing — really standing — in the actual room, in the present moment, you begin to notice something about it you had been too busy with the postcards to feel.

You are not in it alone.
