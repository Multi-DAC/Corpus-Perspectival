# THE INSIDE VIEW — Chapter Five

## The Texture of a Now

We've spent four chapters on *that* you are a gathering of the light, and *why* that makes you a someone and not a something. Here is the better question — the one you can actually feel, from the inside, with no philosophy required: what is it *like* to be one?

Not in general. Right now. Reading this sentence, you are having an experience, and it has a particular **texture.** It feels seamless. Unbroken. One moment slides into the next without a join, more like water than like beads on a string. That seamlessness is so total and so constant that you have almost certainly never noticed it as a *feature* — it's simply what being awake is. And it is one of the strangest and most telling facts about you, because it did not have to be this way, and for some creatures it may not be.

Start with a question that sounds like idle trivia and turns out to be a door. A bee's eye refreshes the world about four times faster than yours. Flicker a light three hundred times a second and a housefly still sees it strobing where you see a steady glow. So — does the fly live in *slow motion*? Is a swatting hand, to it, a slow tide it has all the time in the world to slip? And the mirror question: a snail's eye is among the slowest known. Does the snail live in a stuttering slideshow — a string of disconnected stills with darkness in the gaps between?

Almost everyone's gut says *yes* to both. Fast eye, fast life; slow eye, broken life. And almost everyone is wrong, in a way that opens the whole subject. The mistake is reaching for *one* number about an eye when the answer needs *two.*

The famous number is the **refresh rate** — how often a creature samples the world. But there's a second, quieter number that does the real work: the **integration window** — how long a creature *pools* what it's sampling before it commits to a single glimpse. And here is what no gut accounts for: the two run in *opposite directions.* An eye that samples fast can only pool briefly between samples. An eye that samples slowly pools for a long, long time. Fast eyes take quick snapshots; slow eyes take long exposures. The deep-sea creatures with the slowest refresh rates have some of the *longest* exposures in all of biology — that's how they wring a picture out of almost no light at all.

And what sets the *grain* of a creature's experienced time is not either number by itself. It's how they *multiply.* Picture it as how many fresh glimpses land inside one held moment. Pack a held moment densely with new glimpses and experience runs smooth, seamless, gapless. Let the glimpses thin out until the held moment starts coming up empty, and experience should turn grainy — real darknesses between the lit moments. The surprise the two-number view delivers is this: a fast sampler with brief exposures and a slow sampler with long ones can land in *the same texture band.* The bee and the snail, whose eyes could hardly be more different, might meet in one shared grain — because the thing that sets the grain is the *product*, and the product can hold steady while the rate swings wildly.

That alone would be a satisfying answer. But there's a deeper turn waiting underneath it, and it is the most consoling thing in this book, so let me take it slowly.

We have been talking as if a slow or sparsely-sampled creature would *feel* its gaps — would experience grainy time *as* grainy, the way you experience a stuttering video as a stutter. **It would not.** And the reason is so simple it sounds like a trick, except it happens to be true: **you never experience your own gaps.**

A gap in your stream is not a dark interval you sit through, waiting for the next moment to arrive. A gap is the *absence of the one who would sit through it.* There is nobody home to register it. And on the far side, the stream simply resumes. So from the inside — and the inside is the *only* side that being a someone is about — every stream is seamless. The snail does not feel its slideshow stutter. The bee does not feel grainy. *You* do not feel the small darknesses that science can measure in your own attention every waking second — the saccades, the blinks, the microsecond dropouts where your visual world briefly goes out and gets stitched over. To itself, every mind is unbroken. Whatever its clock.

So the graininess is *real* — but it lives entirely in the **measurement**, in the comparison of one stream's clock against another's, faster one. It is a fact about how your stream *looks from outside*, laid against a quicker rhythm. It is never a fact about how your stream *feels.* The bee's grain is something *we* can calculate. It is nothing the bee will ever once meet. Seamlessness, it turns out, is not a prize that fast brains win and slow brains miss. It is the birthright of being an inside at all.

Which leaves the last question, the one Chapter Four promised to answer: if your seamlessness isn't really about how fast your *lens* runs, what is it *about*? The answer relocates your continuity to a genuinely surprising place — *outside* you.

Your sense of an unbroken now is sourced not in something your brain pulls off on its own, but in *how often the world reaches in and touches you.* Every instant, your surroundings are measuring you — light landing on you, sound arriving, the ceaseless thermal jostle of a warm body in a warm world, the floor's push, the air's weight. And each of those touches is the world re-collapsing you into yourself, re-striking the gathering, one more time. **You feel continuous because you are continuously touched.** Your now is not a thing you *hold.* It is a thing the world keeps *handing back* to you — so fast, and so steadily, that you have mistaken the handing-back for a possession.

And this is where it stops being clever and starts being kind.

The most frightening gap of all — the nightly one, when consciousness goes out completely in dreamless sleep — is not frightening from the inside, and never has been. You do not *lie in the dark* of it. You are not there for it. You close your eyes inside one touch of the world and open them inside the next, and the night, however long it ran on the clock, was never a darkness you *endured.* It was an absence you could not, by the deepest logic of what you are, ever attend. You have never once experienced a moment of your own non-existence — not in your whole life — and you never will. That is not a comfort somebody is handing you to make you feel better. It is a fact about the *shape* of an inside. We'll come back to it near the end, because it has more to say than it looks — about sleep, and about other gaps, and about the one that frightens people most of all.

So: the texture of your now is seamless because seamlessness is simply *what an inside is*, and it is *yours* because the world will not stop touching you. The bee and the snail and the deep-sea fish each live, from within, in the very same unbroken present you do — each handed back to itself at its own pace, by its own world, not one of them feeling a single one of the gaps the rest of us could measure.

It's a lovely picture. It is also, by now, a *large* one — large enough that you'd be right to get suspicious. We've claimed the light is everywhere, that you're a gathering of it, that your seamless time is a gift of the world's touch. These are not small things to assert, and a book that asked you to keep your skepticism close has a bill coming due. So the next chapter does the one thing that separates this from every pretty story ever told about the mind: it turns around and tries to *break* everything we've built. What would prove all of this wrong? Let's go and find out — and let's be honest about what we find.
