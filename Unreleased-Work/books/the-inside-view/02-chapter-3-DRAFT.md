# THE INSIDE VIEW — Chapter Three

## Turning It Over

Go back to the very first question this book asked. *Where does the inside come from?*

Read it again, slowly, because the trap is in the wording — and it is the same trap that swallowed every failed answer of the last chapter.

*Where does it come from* assumes it comes from somewhere. It assumes the inside is, at bottom, **absent** — that the universe starts out dark, built of stuff with nobody home, and that experience is a late arrival, something that has to be brought in, switched on, manufactured. Every theory we watched fail accepted that without a glance. The emergentist says the inside is produced by complexity. The quantum theorist says it is produced by some exotic physics. The illusionist says it is not really produced at all, because it is not really there. They disagree about everything except the one thing none of them thought to doubt: that the raw material of the world is, to begin with, insentient — outsides without insides — and that mind is a thing to be added on top.

That is the hidden assumption. It has been hiding in plain sight since the first sentence of this book, in the word *come.* And it is so deep that for most of us it does not feel like an assumption at all. It feels like the floor.

It is the floor that has been giving way for a hundred years.

Here is why those theories all fail in the same way, like keys cut for the wrong lock. If you begin with ingredients that have no inside, there is no recipe — none, in principle — that adds up to one. Every step of every recipe is just more outside: more structure, more behavior, more parts pushing parts. You can stack outsides to the moon and you will only ever have a taller stack of outside. The feeling never appears in the cake because nobody put any in the bowl. The hard problem is not hard because the answer is subtle. It is hard because we have been trying to bake something out of ingredients from which, by our own stated rules, it cannot be baked.

So turn the assumption over. It costs nothing to try — that is what assumptions are for.

What if the inside was never absent? What if the base layer of the world — whatever the universe is ultimately made of — already has an inside: faint, formless, unimaginably simpler than yours, but *there*? Not dead stuff that somehow learns to feel, but feeling-stuff, of which "dead matter" is merely the view from the outside.

Watch what happens to the century-old problem the instant you do this. It is not solved. It *evaporates.* There is no longer any question of how to get experience from non-experience, because there is no non-experience to start from and nothing to manufacture. The wall that defeated every assault for a hundred years turns out not to have been a wall at all. It was the shadow cast by a single wrong word, and when you turn the word over, the shadow has nothing to fall on. *What is east of the North Pole?* is not a hard geography problem awaiting a clever cartographer. It is a question that stops existing the moment you understand the shape of the globe. The hard problem is exactly that kind of question, and we are about to understand the shape of the globe.

Now — I can hear the objection, because it is the right one to have, and a book that asked you to bring your skepticism had better welcome it when it comes. *This is just mysticism with a clean shirt on. You have "solved" the problem by waving a wand and declaring that everything feels. That is not hard-nosed. That is the softest thing a person could possibly say.*

It is the natural reaction, and it is exactly backwards, and showing you why is the work this chapter exists to do. Because the flip is not the extravagant option. **It is the conservative one** — the move that assumes less, not more. Three things make that true, and none of them require you to believe anything you do not already, on reflection, know.

**First: you have never once met matter.** Not ever. Stop and check this against your own life, because it is the most important sentence in the book and it is simply true. Everything you have ever encountered — every object, every measurement, every result in every experiment in the history of science — arrived to you *as an experience.* The weight of a stone is a felt heaviness in your hand. A reading on an instrument is a pattern of light reaching your eyes. The hardest data of the hardest science is, at the moment you receive it, a conscious event and nothing else. Experience is not the strange thing that physics has to reach out and explain from a safe distance. Experience is the only thing you have ever directly had. Physics is a magnificent *summary* of the patterns in it — abstracted, generalized, stripped of the felt quality so it can be written down and shared. We built that summary, and then we made the oldest mistake there is: we mistook the summary for the world, the map for the territory, and then spent a century baffled about how to get the territory back out of the map. The inside was never the mystery. It was the data all along.

**Second: physics, on its own terms, has a hole in it exactly this shape.** Look closely at what physics actually says about a particle. It tells you its mass — which is to say, how it resists being pushed. Its charge — how it pushes other particles. Its position, its spin, its behavior under forces. Notice that every last one of these is a *relation*: what the thing does, to other things, under conditions. Physics is a flawless account of how the world *behaves.* What it never tells you — what it is not even in the business of telling you — is what the things that are doing all this behaving actually *are,* in themselves, intrinsically, when nothing is looking. There is a blank there, at the very bottom of the most precise science we have: structure described in perfect detail, and no word about the nature of the stuff that has the structure. The substrate view does just one thing with that blank. It fills it with the single piece of intrinsic nature any of us has ever actually been acquainted with — the one we have from the inside, right now: experience. Consciousness is not an awkward extra bolted onto physics. It is the answer to a question physics was already asking and could not answer with its own tools: *yes, but what is all this behavior the behavior of?*

**Third, and simplest: it is cheaper.** The standard picture asks you to believe in two fundamentally different kinds of thing — dead matter, and the experience that somehow rides on it — plus an unexplained bridge between them that no one has ever been able to build. The flip asks you to believe in one kind of thing, seen two ways: from outside, as what physics measures; from inside, as what it is like to be. One substance, two aspects, no bridge, no hard problem. When one picture needs two mysterious ingredients and a miracle to join them, and the other needs one ingredient and no miracle, the knife of good reasoning has only one way to cut. It cuts toward the flip. The mystical-sounding option turns out to be the parsimonious one; the "common-sense" option turns out to be the one carrying the extra, unpayable debt.

There is one more objection, and it is the loudest, so let me meet it before it runs away with you: *Fine — then you are telling me my coffee mug is conscious. My thermostat has an inner life. A rock broods.* And the honest answer is: not in any way you should picture, and the difference is the whole subject of the next chapter, so let me only plant the flag here. To say the inside is present everywhere is **not** to say it is the same everywhere. A glimmer is present in a rock the way warmth is present in a single molecule — real, but so faint, so formless, so utterly without unity or memory or self that calling it "an inner life" badly overstates it. What you have — a rich, bound, self-aware *world* of experience — is to a rock's bare flicker what a symphony is to a single sustained note. Both are sound. Only one is music. The brain's enormous achievement is not to *create* the inside out of nothing; it is to *gather* an unfathomable number of faint insides into one vast, unified, self-reflecting now. The light is everywhere. The brain is what builds a *lens.* But that is Chapter Four, and I am getting ahead of us.

For now, stand still in what just happened, because it is worth the vertigo. We did not add anything exotic to the universe. We took something away — a single buried assumption, the one that said the world is dead until proven otherwise — and the hardest problem in the history of thought went out with it, the way a draft stops the instant you find the open window. What is left is stranger and quieter and far more reasonable than the picture we started with:

You are not a lump of insensate matter that has, against all the odds and all the laws, been coaxed into feeling. You never were. You are the universe on the inside of one of its own knots — a place where the cosmos, which has had an interior all along, has folded itself complex enough to turn and look at its own face. The matter is real. Your brain is real, and it matters enormously, and we are going to need every gram of it. But the matter is the *outside* of you. The inside — the part that is reading this, the part that was the one undeniable thing back in Chapter One — that was never produced, because it was never missing.

It was what the universe was made of the whole time.

Now let's go find out why, if that's true, your coffee mug still isn't anybody — and why you so unmistakably are.
